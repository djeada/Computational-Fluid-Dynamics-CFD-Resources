# Dynamic Mode Decomposition as a Reduced-Order Model

Dynamic mode decomposition (DMD) fits a linear model to a sequence of snapshots and decomposes the data into spatial modes that each oscillate at one frequency and grow or decay at one rate. Seen from the reduced-order modeling side, it is a non-intrusive linear ROM in POD coordinates: the reduced operator is fitted to data instead of projected from the governing equations. This note treats DMD in that role, together with its use for modal analysis: how it relates to Galerkin projection, to the Koopman operator and to linear stability, how modes are ranked and selected, the optimized and controlled variants, what limits its predictions, the practical choices (rank, sampling interval, mean subtraction, noise) that decide whether its eigenvalues mean anything, and how it compares with POD and spectral POD (SPOD).

The data-driven side, including the derivation of exact DMD, Koopman theory, extended DMD and a worked example with known eigenvalues, is in [Modeling Flow Dynamics](../../machine_learning/flow/flow_dynamics.md), and the implementation is the [DMD script](../../../scripts/algorithms/dynamic_mode_decomposition/). The projection-based alternative is [POD-Galerkin Projection](pod_galerkin_projection.md).

## Problem Statement

Given $m$ snapshots $\mathbf{x}_k = \mathbf{x}(t_0 + k\Delta t) \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $k = 0, \dots, m - 1$, sampled at a constant interval $\Delta t$ from a CFD run or an experiment (PIV fields, pressure probes), find $r \ll n$ modes $\boldsymbol{\phi}_j \in \mathbb{C}^n$, eigenvalues $\lambda_j \in \mathbb{C}$ and amplitudes $b_j \in \mathbb{C}$ such that

```math
\mathbf{x}_k \approx \sum_{j=1}^{r} b_j\lambda_j^k\boldsymbol{\phi}_j = \Phi\Lambda^k\mathbf{b},
\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
\mathbf{x}_{k+1} \approx A\mathbf{x}_k \text{ with } \mathrm{rank}(A) = r
```

Each eigenvalue gives a continuous-time growth rate and frequency, $\omega_j = \ln(\lambda_j)/\Delta t = g_j + 2\pi\mathrm{i}f_j$. The decomposition is used in two ways: for modal analysis, where the eigenvalues and modes should be properties of the flow (shedding frequency, growth rate of an instability, shape of a global mode), and as a ROM, where $\Phi\Lambda^k\mathbf{b}$ is evaluated for $k \ge m$ to predict the flow. It is solved when the reconstruction error on the data is at the noise level and the eigenvalues of interest do not change when $r$, $\Delta t$ or the record length are varied. What makes it hard: a linear model cannot represent nonlinear features such as a stable limit cycle or a transient with a changing mean flow; noise in the snapshots biases the eigenvalues; the frequencies that can be identified are limited by the sampling interval; and DMD modes are not ranked by energy, so it is not obvious which of them matter.

## Exact DMD in Brief

With the shifted data matrices $`X = [\mathbf{x}_0 \cdots \mathbf{x}_{m-2}]`$ and $`X' = [\mathbf{x}_1 \cdots \mathbf{x}_{m-1}]`$, DMD approximates the least-squares operator $A = X'X^+$ without forming it. The exact DMD algorithm (Tu et al. 2014) computes the rank-$`r`$ SVD $X \approx U_r\Sigma_rV_r^{\mathsf{H}}$, projects $A$ onto the POD modes $U_r$,

$$
\tilde{A} = U_r^{\mathsf{H}}X'V_r\Sigma_r^{-1} \in \mathbb{C}^{r \times r}
$$

solves $\tilde{A}W = W\Lambda$, and forms the modes $\Phi = X'V_r\Sigma_r^{-1}W$, which are eigenvectors of $X'X^+$. The cost is one thin SVD, $O(nm^2)$, plus an $r \times r$ eigenproblem. The original algorithm of Schmid (2010) uses the projected modes $U_rW$ instead, which agree with the exact ones when the columns of $X'$ lie in the range of $X$. The algorithm, its eigenvalue interpretation and a worked example are in [Modeling Flow Dynamics](../../machine_learning/flow/flow_dynamics.md).

## DMD as a Linear ROM

Writing the snapshots in POD coordinates, $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_k = U_r^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{x}_k$, the matrix $\tilde{A}$ is the least-squares solution of

$$
\tilde{A} = \arg\min_{B \in \mathbb{C}^{r \times r}} \sum_{k=0}^{m-2} \lVert
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1} - B\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_k \rVert_2^2
$$

so DMD is an $r$-dimensional linear ROM, $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1} = \tilde{A}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_k$, in the same coordinates a POD-Galerkin model would use. The difference is where the reduced operator comes from:

|  | POD-Galerkin | DMD |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reduced operator | $U_r^TA_hU_r$ for a linear FOM $A_h$; constant, linear and quadratic terms for Navier–Stokes | best linear fit to the snapshot pairs |
| Needs the solver | yes (intrusive) | no; works on experimental data |
| Model form | the structure of the equations, including nonlinearity | linear, time-invariant |
| Time step | any; the ROM is an ODE | fixed to the sampling interval $\Delta t$ |
| Behaviour beyond the data | follows the projected physics, may drift | sum of exponentials, extrapolated |

For data generated by a linear system $\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = A_h\mathbf{x}_k$ whose snapshots span an invariant subspace of $A_h$, DMD recovers the exact eigenvalues of $A_h$ on that subspace, and $\tilde{A}$ coincides with the Galerkin operator $U_r^{\mathsf{H}}A_hU_r$. Schmid's derivation makes the link to linear stability explicit: DMD is a Rayleigh–Ritz approximation of $A_h$ in the Krylov subspace spanned by the snapshots, as in the Arnoldi method, but built from data alone. Applied to small perturbations of a steady base flow, it gives approximations of the leading global stability modes.

For nonlinear dynamics, Rowley et al. (2009) showed that DMD approximates the Koopman mode decomposition, the expansion of the state in eigenfunctions of the linear operator that advances observables along trajectories. On a limit cycle with fundamental frequency $f_0$, the Koopman eigenvalues are $e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}kf_0\Delta t}$, and DMD returns the Fourier modes of the shedding frequency and its harmonics. What the linear model cannot do is attract: a limit cycle is represented by neutral eigenvalues on the unit circle, a nearby initial state stays on a nearby cycle instead of converging, and any error in $\lvert\lambda_j\rvert$ grows or shrinks the predicted oscillation exponentially.

### Prediction and Its Limits

The DMD prediction $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k = \Phi\Lambda^k\mathbf{b}$ for $k \ge m$ is an extrapolation of exponentials. An error $\delta g$ in a growth rate changes the predicted amplitude of that mode by the factor $`e^{\delta g\,t}`$ after a time $t$, and an error $\delta f$ in a frequency shifts its phase by $`2\pi\,\delta f\,t`$, so even small eigenvalue errors limit the useful horizon to a few times the record length. In the [DMD script](../../../scripts/algorithms/dynamic_mode_decomposition/), noise biases the growth rate of a decaying packet from $-0.150$ to $-0.205$ s $`^{-1}`$; 10 s beyond the record that is a factor $e^{-0.55} \approx 0.58$ in its amplitude, and the forecast error doubles from 6% to 12%. DMD forecasts are reliable for linear or nearly linear dynamics (small perturbations, forced linear response) and for periodic flows over a few periods, not for transients in which the mean flow changes, for intermittent events, or for chaotic or turbulent flows, whose continuous spectrum no finite set of exponentials represents.

## Mode Amplitudes and Selection

The amplitudes are usually fitted to the first snapshot, $\mathbf{b} = \Phi^+\mathbf{x}_0$, which makes them sensitive to the noise in that one snapshot. Jovanović, Schmid and Nichols (2014) instead choose $\mathbf{b}$ to minimize the reconstruction error over all snapshots,

```math
\min_{\mathbf{b}} \left\lVert X - \Phi\,\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{b})\,V_{\mathrm{and}} \right\rVert_F^2,
\qquad
V_{\mathrm{and}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \lambda_1 & \cdots & \lambda_1^{m-2} \\ \vdots & & & \vdots \\ 1 & \lambda_r & \cdots & \lambda_r^{m-2} \end{pmatrix}
```

which is a convex quadratic problem in $\mathbf{b}$ with a closed-form solution. Unlike POD modes, DMD modes are not orthogonal and are not ordered by energy, so a large $\lvert b_j \rvert$ can belong to a strongly damped mode that disappears after a few snapshots, and two nearly parallel modes can have large amplitudes that cancel. Common rankings use $\lvert b_j \rvert\lVert\boldsymbol{\phi}_j\rVert$, the time-integrated contribution $`\sum_k \lvert b_j\lambda_j^k \rvert\lVert\boldsymbol{\phi}_j\rVert`$, or the distance of $\lambda_j$ from the unit circle.

Sparsity-promoting DMD (Jovanović et al. 2014) makes the selection systematic by adding an $\ell_1$ penalty $\gamma\sum_j\lvert b_j \rvert$ to the amplitude problem. Increasing $\gamma$ drives more amplitudes to zero; for each $\gamma$ the surviving amplitudes are then refitted without the penalty, and the trade-off between the number of modes and the reconstruction error is read off a curve, as for any sparse regression.

## Optimized DMD

Exact DMD fits each pair $`(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{x}_{k+1})`$ separately, so noise in $X$ enters as errors in the regressors. Optimized DMD (Askham and Kutz 2018) fits the whole trajectory at once,

```math
\min_{\boldsymbol{\omega} \in \mathbb{C}^r,\ B \in \mathbb{C}^{r \times n}} \left\lVert X^T - \Psi(\boldsymbol{\omega})B \right\rVert_F,
\qquad
\Psi(\boldsymbol{\omega})_{kj} = e^{\omega_jt_k}
```

with the continuous-time eigenvalues $\omega_j$ and the mode matrix $B$, the amplitudes absorbed into the modes. For fixed $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ the problem is linear in $B$, which variable projection eliminates, leaving a nonlinear least-squares problem in the $r$ eigenvalues only. Optimized DMD is much less biased by noise, accepts non-uniform sampling times, and can be started from the exact DMD eigenvalues; its cost is an iterative optimization, and it can converge to local minima.

## DMD with Control

For an actuated flow with inputs $`\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_k`$ (jet velocity, plasma actuator voltage), DMD with control (Proctor, Brunton and Kutz 2016) fits $`\mathbf{x}_{k+1} \approx A\mathbf{x}_k + B\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_k`$ by the least-squares solution $[A\ B] = X'[X;\ \Upsilon]^+$, computed in a truncated SVD as before. Without the input term, DMD would attribute the forced response to the unforced dynamics and return wrong eigenvalues. The fitted pair $(A, B)$, projected onto the POD modes of the output space, is a linear state-space ROM that can be used directly for controller design.

## Practical Choices

### Truncation Rank

The rank $r$ sets how many modes the model has. Too small, and dynamics are missing; too large, and DMD fits the noise with spurious, often strongly damped, eigenvalues. Look for a gap in the singular values of $X$ above the noise floor, use an optimal hard threshold for the singular values (for an $n \times n$ matrix with white noise of standard deviation $\sigma$, $`\tau = (4/\sqrt{3})\sqrt{n}\,\sigma`$; Gavish and Donoho, *IEEE Transactions on Information Theory*, 2014, give the formula for other aspect ratios and for unknown noise), and check that the eigenvalues of interest do not move as $r$ is increased by a few. A real oscillation needs a conjugate pair, so a single travelling wave needs $r = 2$ and a standing wave sampled at one point needs time-delay stacking (a Hankel matrix of shifted copies of the data) to supply the missing rank.

### Sampling Interval and Record Length

The discrete eigenvalue determines the frequency only modulo the sampling frequency, because $\arg\lambda_j = 2\pi f_j\Delta t$ is defined modulo $2\pi$. Frequencies above the Nyquist limit $1/(2\Delta t)$ fold back into the resolved range (Exercise 2), so $\Delta t$ must resolve the highest frequency present in the data, not only the highest of interest, or the signal must be low-pass filtered first. Very small $\Delta t$ is not better: all eigenvalues crowd near $\lambda = 1$, and growth rates $\ln\lvert\lambda\rvert/\Delta t$ become the ratio of two small numbers. Ten to thirty samples per period of the highest relevant frequency is a common compromise. The record should cover several periods of the slowest oscillation of interest and, for transients, the whole transient.

### Mean Subtraction

Subtracting the temporal mean, routine in POD, changes DMD fundamentally. Chen, Tu and Rowley (2012) showed that for mean-subtracted data the companion-matrix form of DMD returns eigenvalues on the unit circle at the discrete Fourier frequencies $k/(m\Delta t)$, so DMD reduces to a temporal discrete Fourier transform and loses all growth-rate information (Exercise 3). Apply DMD to the raw snapshots, in which the mean appears as a mode with $\lambda \approx 1$, or subtract a known steady base flow (a fixed point of the equations) rather than the sample mean. The time average of a finite record is not a property of the dynamics and should not be removed.

### Noise Bias

Least squares treats $X$ as exact and puts all error in $X'$. Noise in $X$ therefore biases the fitted operator towards zero, which pulls the eigenvalues inside the unit circle and makes neutral oscillations look damped. For a neutral rotation sampled with white noise of variance $\sigma_n^2$ per component and signal variance $\sigma_s^2$ per component, $\lvert\lambda\rvert \approx 1/(1 + \sigma_n^2/\sigma_s^2)$ (Exercise 4); Dawson, Hemati, Williams and Rowley (2016) derived the general first-order bias. Their remedies, and others, are:

- forward–backward DMD, which also fits the time-reversed data, $X \approx A_bX'$, and combines the estimates as $(AA_b^{-1})^{1/2}$, so that the biases cancel to first order;
- total-least-squares DMD (Hemati et al. 2017), which projects $X$ and $X'$ onto the leading $r$ right singular vectors of the stacked matrix $[X;\ X']$ before the exact DMD step, treating both matrices as noisy;
- optimized DMD, which fits the whole trajectory.

Recording more snapshots does not help, because the bias does not average out.

## DMD, POD and SPOD

POD, DMD and spectral POD answer different questions about the same data.

|  | POD | DMD | SPOD |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Question | which spatial structures carry the most energy? | which structures evolve with one frequency and growth rate? | which structures carry the most energy at each frequency? |
| Modes | orthonormal, real, ranked by energy | non-orthogonal, complex, no natural ranking | orthonormal at each frequency, ranked by energy at that frequency |
| Time dependence of a mode | broadband coefficient $a_j(t)$ | $e^{\omega_jt}$ | $e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}ft}$ |
| Data assumption | none | linear dynamics or a discrete Koopman spectrum | statistically stationary flow, many realizations or a long record |
| Growth rates and transients | no | yes | no |
| Robustness to noise | good; noise ends up in the trailing modes | biased eigenvalues | good; averaged over blocks |

Spectral POD, introduced by Lumley for turbulence and developed as a practical tool by Towne, Schmidt and Colonius (2018), is the eigendecomposition of the cross-spectral density tensor of a stationary flow. It is computed with Welch's method:

1. Split the record into $N_b$ blocks of $N_f$ snapshots each, usually with 50% overlap, and apply a window (for example Hann) to each block.
2. Take the temporal FFT of each block, giving at each frequency $f_l$ one Fourier realization $\hat{\mathbf{q}}_l^{(i)}$ per block.
3. At each frequency, form $\hat{Q}_l = [\hat{\mathbf{q}}_l^{(1)} \cdots \hat{\mathbf{q}}_l^{(N_b)}]$ and solve the $N_b \times N_b$ eigenproblem $N_b^{-1}\hat{Q}_l^{\mathsf{H}}W\hat{Q}_l\Theta_l = \Theta_l\Lambda_l$ with the quadrature weights $W$.
4. The SPOD modes are $`\Psi_l = N_b^{-1/2}\hat{Q}_l\Theta_l\Lambda_l^{-1/2}`$, and the eigenvalues $`\lambda_{l,1} \ge \lambda_{l,2} \ge \cdots`$ give the energy spectrum of each mode family.

Towne et al. (2018) showed that SPOD modes are optimally averaged DMD modes: DMD of one realization of a stationary turbulent flow returns modes that vary from one realization to the next, whereas SPOD averages over the blocks and converges as $N_b$ grows. A large gap between $\lambda_{l,1}$ and $\lambda_{l,2}$ at a frequency indicates a low-rank, coherent mechanism at that frequency, such as wavepackets in a jet. The block length sets the frequency resolution $1/(N_f\Delta t)$ and the number of blocks the statistical convergence, a trade-off fixed by the record length (Exercise 5).

As a rule: use POD for compression and Galerkin bases, DMD for transients, stability problems, periodic flows and short-term linear prediction, and SPOD for the coherent structures of statistically stationary turbulence.

## Key Takeaways

- DMD is a linear ROM in POD coordinates whose operator $\tilde{A} = U_r^{\mathsf{H}}X'V_r\Sigma_r^{-1}$ is fitted to snapshot pairs; for linear data it coincides with the Galerkin operator and recovers exact eigenvalues.
- Each mode has one frequency and growth rate; a linear model represents a limit cycle only by neutral eigenvalues and cannot attract, so predictions are limited by eigenvalue errors.
- Amplitudes fitted to all snapshots, sparsity-promoting selection and optimized DMD make the decomposition less sensitive to noise and the choice of modes systematic.
- Choose $r$ from the singular value spectrum, sample well above the Nyquist rate of everything in the data, do not subtract the sample mean, and correct the noise bias with forward–backward, total-least-squares or optimized DMD.
- For statistically stationary turbulence, SPOD gives converged, energy-ranked modes at each frequency where DMD gives realization-dependent ones.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** A DMD eigenvalue computed from snapshots taken every $\Delta t = 0.01$ s is $`\lambda = 0.98\,e^{0.3\mathrm{i}}`$. Find the growth rate, the frequency in Hz and the time in which the mode amplitude halves.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$g = \ln(0.98)/0.01 = -2.02$ s $`^{-1}`$ and $f = 0.3/(2\pi \times 0.01) = 4.77$ Hz. The amplitude halves after $\ln 2/2.02 = 0.343$ s, about 1.6 periods.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** A flow contains an oscillation at 70 Hz and is sampled every $\Delta t = 0.01$ s. At what frequency does DMD report it, and what sampling interval would be needed to identify it correctly?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$\arg\lambda = 2\pi \times 70 \times 0.01 = 1.4\pi$, which is equivalent to $-0.6\pi$. The conjugate pair therefore appears at $0.6\pi/(2\pi \times 0.01) = 30$ Hz: the 70 Hz oscillation is aliased to $100 - 70 = 30$ Hz, below the Nyquist limit of 50 Hz. To resolve it, $\Delta t < 1/140$ s is required, and in practice $\Delta t \le 1/700$ s (ten samples per period).

</details>

**Exercise 3.** Take $m = 3$ mean-subtracted snapshots $\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2$ of any signal, so that $\mathbf{x}_0 + \mathbf{x}_1 + \mathbf{x}_2 = \mathbf{0}$, with $\mathbf{x}_0$ and $\mathbf{x}_1$ linearly independent. In the companion-matrix formulation of DMD, the last snapshot is written as $\mathbf{x}_2 = c_0\mathbf{x}_0 + c_1\mathbf{x}_1$, and the DMD eigenvalues are the roots of $z^2 - c_1z - c_0$. Find them and interpret the result.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Mean subtraction gives $\mathbf{x}_2 = -\mathbf{x}_0 - \mathbf{x}_1$, so $c_0 = c_1 = -1$ and the polynomial is $z^2 + z + 1$, with roots $z = e^{\pm 2\pi\mathrm{i}/3}$. These are on the unit circle at the frequency $1/(3\Delta t)$, whatever the data were, a decaying transient or a growing instability. With $m$ snapshots the same argument gives the $m$-th roots of unity other than 1, the frequencies of the discrete Fourier transform, which is the result of Chen, Tu and Rowley (2012).

</details>

**Exercise 4.** A neutral oscillation $\mathbf{x}_k = (\cos(0.3k), \sin(0.3k))$ is sampled at $\Delta t = 0.01$ s with independent Gaussian noise of standard deviation $\sigma_n = 0.1$ on each component. Estimate the modulus of the DMD eigenvalues for a long record, and the spurious growth rate. How would total-least-squares DMD change the result?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For a long record, $`X'X^{\mathsf{H}}/m \to R\,C`$ and $XX^{\mathsf{H}}/m \to C + \sigma_n^2I$, where $R$ is the rotation by 0.3 rad and $C = \frac{1}{2}I$ the signal covariance. The fitted operator tends to $`R\,C(C + \sigma_n^2I)^{-1} = R/(1 + 2\sigma_n^2)`$, so $\lvert\lambda\rvert \approx 1/1.02 = 0.9804$ and the growth rate is $\ln(0.9804)/0.01 = -1.98$ s $`^{-1}`$, although the oscillation is neutral. A simulation with $2 \times 10^4$ snapshots gives $\lvert\lambda\rvert = 0.9804$. Total-least-squares DMD, which first projects $X$ and $X'$ onto the two leading right singular vectors of $[X;\ X']$, gives $\lvert\lambda\rvert = 1.00000$ for the same data.

</details>

**Exercise 5.** An SPOD analysis uses $10^4$ snapshots sampled at 2 kHz, blocks of $N_f = 256$ snapshots and 50% overlap. How many blocks are there, what are the frequency resolution and the highest resolved frequency, and what changes if the block length is doubled?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

With an overlap of 128 snapshots, $N_b = \lfloor (10^4 - 256)/128 \rfloor + 1 = 77$ blocks. The frequency resolution is $2000/256 = 7.81$ Hz and the Nyquist frequency 1000 Hz. Doubling the block length to 512 halves the resolution to 3.91 Hz but leaves only $\lfloor (10^4 - 512)/256 \rfloor + 1 = 38$ blocks, so the eigenvalue estimates at each frequency are averaged over half as many realizations and converge more slowly.

</details>
